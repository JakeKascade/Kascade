# Content architecture and narrative decisions

The reader is Matt and Sam at Real Coder. They proposed the partnership first, so the page reads as a considered response: it leads with what Real Coder and its clients gain, settles the “will they compete with us?” question early, shows exactly what they would be introducing, and only then explains the money in detail.

## Material changes from the supplied narrative, and why

1. **Added a “recognise the client” panel before the packages** (panel 3). The brief’s goal is for Real Coder to *immediately recognise which existing clients could benefit*. Prices alone don’t do that; client signals (“about to brief a new website, with a dated or DIY logo”) do, and they tie each offer back to Real Coder’s own work.
2. **Commission appears next to every package**, computed from the catalogue (“Real Coder’s 10%: $345 a month, up to $4,140 over the first 12 paid months”). The supplied order held earnings until panel 6–7, which made the services read as Kascade’s sales pitch. Showing the return in context answers “what’s in it for us?” without aggressive language, and the full mechanics still follow in chapter 3.
3. **Branding is a comparison table, not two cards.** The commercial master stresses that Professional isn’t “a refresh plus $2,000”; a row-by-row comparison makes the difference visible in seconds.
4. **The agency marketing add-on moved from the Creative Partner panel to Real Estate.** It can only be added to a real estate retainer, so it belongs with those tiers; the Creative Partner panel keeps a note about bespoke embedded engagements.
5. **The listing lifecycle diagram** turns the most important (and most misunderstood) scope rule — a listing is counted once, at brief acceptance — into a picture.
6. **The referral flow uses the five steps requested** (introduction → consultation → client approves → client pays → Real Coder earns), keeps “Real Coder continues its web/SEO work throughout” as a parallel lane, and holds the payment split at “Awaiting payment” until the client-pays step, so the animation never implies commission before payment. Kascade pays the commission, from the cleared payment; Real Coder never pays or controls scope.
7. **Gains and terms are separate panels**, and the terms end with an honest **“To agree together”** list. The 12-paid-month cap differs from Real Coder’s own offer of ongoing SEO commission; the brief says to discuss that rather than disguise it, so the page says so plainly.
8. **Positioning line** (“Better brands. Stronger digital experiences. Shared opportunities.”) captions the hero diagram rather than competing with the headline.
9. **Copy is shortened throughout**, and long lists (inclusions, conditions, exclusions, methodology) sit behind disclosures — always expanded in print, never truncated.

## Calculator design

- **First impression:** opens on a simple, realistic case (one Growth agency + one Professional brand = $4,540) with a single large “Real Coder could earn” figure; one-off and recurring are split beneath it with their own counts.
- **Builder:** each line is a signed-and-paid referral — service, number of clients, month, add-ons, how long a retainer pays. Each line shows its own contribution.
- **Three illustrative scenarios** load as editable lines, so their assumptions are always visible.
- **Negotiation settings** (rate, retainer period, 24-month view, deposit/balance payments) are behind a disclosure and flagged with a “Testing settings” badge whenever they differ from the proposal.
- **The header Scenario button** opens the same builder in a drawer from anywhere on the page; the summary in the header updates live.
- **Kascade figures are secondary** and labelled as revenue before expenses — never profit.

## Copy principles

Plain, collaborative, specific. “We”, “your clients”. No superlatives, no guarantees, no invented results, testimonials, case studies or Real Coder approvals. Every proposed term is marked *Proposed* or *Provisional* where it appears.
